# Content Craft

Design alone won't save bad writing. This file is the writing half of the skill.

## The premium-content principles

1. **Specificity beats generality.** "She drove a black car" is generic. "She drove a 2003 Volvo with a child seat in the back and an empty coffee cup wedged in the door pocket" puts you in the scene. Premium writing chooses concrete nouns.
2. **Cut every sentence that could be cut.** A book that's 60,000 words and sharp is more premium than 90,000 words padded out. Length is not value.
3. **Vary sentence rhythm.** Long, then short. Long, then short. Then a long one to settle the reader. A page of 20-word sentences feels like wading; a page of 4-word sentences feels like being shouted at. Mix.
4. **Show one thing per paragraph.** A paragraph that pivots between three ideas is a paragraph that should be three paragraphs.
5. **The reader trusts you the moment you tell them something they thought no one else knew.** One sharp insight in chapter 1 buys the whole book.
6. **Don't write what the reader could have written.** If your sentence could be in any book on the topic, delete it.

## Voice — three dials to set, once, in chapter 1

Set these three on the first page and don't drift.

| Dial | Options |
|---|---|
| **Distance** | Close (we hear the narrator's pulse) ↔ Far (a calm overview) |
| **Formality** | Conversational (contractions, slang) ↔ Formal (no contractions, full clauses) |
| **Heat** | Warm (you, we, asides, humor) ↔ Cool (third person, no asides) |

Examples:
- *Atomic Habits* — close, conversational, warm.
- *Sapiens* — far, formal, cool.
- *Rework* — close, conversational, hot (almost confrontational).

A premium book is recognizable from one page because the dials don't move.

## Structure — the spine of a non-fiction book

A premium non-fiction book has a structural skeleton that the reader can feel even if they can't articulate it. Pick one of these and stick to it:

### 1. The Argument

Thesis → evidence → counter-argument → reframe → call to action.
Best for: persuasive non-fiction, business strategy, critique.

### 2. The Journey

Origin → obstacle → discovery → transformation → return with a gift.
Best for: memoir, founder stories, "what I learned" books.

### 3. The Field Guide

Map the territory → key concepts → tools → case studies → reference.
Best for: how-to, technical, methodology books.

### 4. The Frame Set

Frame 1 → frame 2 → frame 3 → … → synthesis.
Best for: ideas books where each chapter is a self-contained mental model.

### 5. The Investigation

Mystery → false leads → real lead → climax → aftermath.
Best for: longform journalism, narrative non-fiction, true crime.

Decide which one **before writing chapter 2**. Most amateur non-fiction drifts because no skeleton was chosen.

## The chapter as a unit

A premium chapter has internal architecture too. Five-part structure that works for almost any non-fiction:

1. **Hook (½ – 1 page)** — A scene, a surprising fact, a question, or an anecdote. Earns the reader's next 20 minutes.
2. **Promise (1 paragraph)** — The thing this chapter will teach or show, stated plainly. "This chapter is about why your pricing page is the wrong place to set price."
3. **Body (the middle)** — The substance. Two to four sub-sections, each with a heading, each ending with a beat the reader can take with them.
4. **Pivot (1 paragraph)** — The reader has the thing now. What changes? What does the next chapter need to address that follows from this one?
5. **Closer (3–5 sentences)** — A single image, a single line, an echo of the hook. The reader closes the chapter and remembers one phrase.

For fiction: hook → rising action → midpoint reversal → climax → cliffhanger or breath. Same architecture; different vocabulary.

## Sentence-level craft — five fixes that lift any draft

Run these passes on every chapter. They'll cut 10–20% of the word count and double the perceived quality.

1. **Strip the throat-clearing.** Find every "It's important to note that", "In this chapter we'll", "What's interesting is", "As we'll see". Delete. Start sentences with the noun.
2. **Replace abstractions with images.** "Improve customer experience" → "make the receipt arrive before the customer leaves the store". Find every abstract noun and ask: what does this look like?
3. **Cut adverbs.** "Walked quickly" → "hurried" or "ran". "Said angrily" → "snapped". The verb usually carries the manner.
4. **Find the buried verb.** "Make the decision to" → "decide". "Have a conversation about" → "talk about". "Provide an explanation" → "explain".
5. **Read aloud.** Where you stumble, the reader stumbles. Fix.

## Microcopy

The non-body text. Easy to leave generic; high return on attention.

- **Subtitle**: not a tagline. A subtitle should narrow the title. "How to Build a Premium Brand: A 12-Step Playbook for Founders Without a Marketing Team."
- **Chapter titles**: short, concrete, ideally evocative. "Pricing" is fine. "The Price Is Not the Number" is better. Avoid clever puns that hide what the chapter is about.
- **Section headers within chapters**: 3–6 words, descriptive, in sentence case (not Title Case unless the whole book uses it). Should answer "what's in this section?"
- **Pull quotes**: not the most quotable line — the most clarifying one. The pull quote should make a reader who's skimming stop and read the surrounding paragraph.
- **Captions**: never just describe the image. Add a fact, a date, a source. The image is *what*; the caption is *so what*.
- **Dedication**: one line. Personal, not generic. "For M., who knew first" is better than "To my family, for their support."
- **Acknowledgments**: write them like you mean them. Use names. Tell one story per person if you can.

## Common smells

If the manuscript has any of these, fix before designing:

- Every chapter starts with a quote from a famous person. (Use one or two epigraphs in the whole book, not one per chapter.)
- Every chapter ends with a bulleted "key takeaways" list. (One can be useful; using it everywhere flattens the prose into corporate-blog tone.)
- Sub-headings phrased as questions throughout. (Once or twice is fine; as a pattern it feels like SEO content.)
- Numbers spelled out inconsistently. (Pick a rule: spell out one through nine, numerals for 10 and up. Or: spell out only at the start of a sentence. Pick one.)
- Inconsistent serial comma. (Use it. Always.)
- Em-dashes vs hyphens vs en-dashes used interchangeably. (—, –, -. Each has a job. Keep them straight.)

## How this skill should help with content

- If the user pastes a draft and asks for "premium polish", run the five sentence-level fixes and cite specific changes — not "tighten throughout".
- If the user asks for a chapter, ask first which structural skeleton (Argument / Journey / Field Guide / Frame Set / Investigation) and which voice dials, before writing.
- Never write filler. If you don't have something specific to say in a paragraph, the paragraph shouldn't exist. Tell the user that instead of generating fluff.
