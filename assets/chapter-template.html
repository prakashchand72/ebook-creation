The Quiet Year

Patience is also a form of action. Auguste Rodin

It was raining the morning the letter arrived, and that detail mattered more than anything else that happened that week — because it was the kind of rain that made you stay inside, and staying inside was when she finally read it.

She had been ignoring the envelope for six days. Six days of seeing it on the kitchen counter, six days of moving it from the counter to the table to the stack of bills, and back. The envelope was thicker than the letters that bring good news, and thinner than the letters that bring bad. Ambiguous, almost considerate.

The first paragraph she read, twice, was about what wasn't going to happen — a list of things being taken off the table. The second paragraph was about timing. The third was about what came next.

What the year was for

The year that followed had a shape she couldn't have predicted. It was, in retrospect, organized around three quiet decisions, each made in a room without an audience. The first was about the work. The second was about the house. The third — which surprised her most — was about who she would tell, and in what order.

The first decision was about the work. The second was about the house. The third surprised her most.

She kept a notebook for the year. Small, the size of a passport. She wrote in it only on Sundays, and only one page each Sunday. By the end of the year there were fifty-two pages — which she read, in order, on a long flight in the spring of the next year, and which had, by then, become the outline of this book.

Three decisions

Each decision had the same structure. A long period of avoidance. A short window of clarity. A single conversation that committed her to it. And then — and this is the part that changed how she works now — a deliberate stretch of doing nothing about it.

  1. Avoidance — usually three to six weeks of carrying the question without addressing it directly.
  2. Clarity — a single hour, often early in the morning, when the answer is obvious.
  3. Commitment — one short conversation with one person.
  4. Quiet — at least two weeks of not telling anyone else, not announcing, not changing externally.

Insight

The "quiet" stage is the one most people skip. It is also the one that distinguishes a decision that holds from one that quietly reverses itself in week three.

The work decision

The work decision was the easiest, in the end. She had known the answer for nearly a year. What she had not known was how to make the answer compatible with everything else — the mortgage, the team, the half-finished project that was already on calendars for the spring. The clarity, when it came, was that the answer didn't have to be compatible. It only had to be true.

The opposite of a good decision is not a bad decision. It is a decision made before the question has finished forming.

The house decision

The house decision was harder for a reason that didn't become clear until much later: the house was the only one of the three things that had no alternative. The work had an alternative — other work. The third decision had an alternative — different people. The house was a single object1, and to decide about it was to decide about it. There was no second house in reserve.

Work 6 1 3

What changed, by the end of the year, was not the decisions themselves. It was the noticing — the ability to feel a decision forming in the avoidance stage, and to leave it alone. To not, prematurely, name it. To not perform it on the way to making it.

That is what the next chapter is about: how to feel a decision forming. What it feels like in the body, in the calendar, in the small frictions that show up first in language. Because the work of making a quiet decision begins long before the decision is named.

The rain stopped on the seventh day. By then the letter was already answered, though it would be another month before she said so out loud.
